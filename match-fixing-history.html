How Match-Fixing Scandals Changed Cricket Forever

From Hansie Cronje's downfall in 2000 to the spot-fixing case at Lord's a decade later — the two scandals that forced cricket to rebuild its system of trust from scratch.

By Your Name

Cricket has always carried an air of gentlemanly trust — a sport built on the honour system as much as the rulebook. That reputation was shattered in April 2000, when Delhi police charged South Africa's captain, Hansie Cronje, with fixing one-day matches against India. It was a name nobody expected. Cronje was one of the most respected leaders in the game, a born-again Christian known for his discipline, not his greed.

At first, Cronje denied everything, insisting he'd never spoken to teammates about throwing a game. But the case unravelled fast. Fellow players began to testify against him at South Africa's King Commission of Inquiry. Herschelle Gibbs admitted he'd been offered money to underperform in an ODI. Other teammates confirmed Cronje had floated offers to lose matches years earlier, for sums running into hundreds of thousands of dollars. Within months, Cronje was banned for life. He died in a plane crash two years later, his legacy forever split between talent and betrayal.

The Cronje scandal did more than end one man's career — it forced cricket's authorities to admit that fixing wasn't a rumour, it was a system. The International Cricket Council responded by creating its Anti-Corruption Unit, led initially by a former Scotland Yard commissioner, to investigate players, agents and bookmakers across the sport.

A decade later, cricket learned the problem hadn't gone away — it had just gotten more precise. In 2010, a British newspaper's sting operation exposed a new kind of fixing: not throwing an entire match, but manipulating small, specific moments within one. Pakistan's captain Salman Butt, along with bowlers Mohammad Asif and a teenage Mohammad Amir, were caught arranging deliberate no-balls at pre-agreed points during a Test match at Lord's, in exchange for payments from an agent working with bookmakers. The scandal became known as "spot-fixing" — proof that even a single ball, not just a full game, could be bought.

All three players were banned by the ICC and later jailed by a London court. Amir, only 19 when the scandal broke, would eventually serve his ban, return to international cricket, and become one of the more sympathetic figures in the saga — young, talented, and arguably manipulated by senior teammates and an agent.

Together, the Cronje and spot-fixing cases reshaped how cricket protects itself. Anti-corruption officials now travel with international teams. Players undergo mandatory education on approaches from bookmakers. Phones are monitored during major tournaments, and suspicious betting patterns are flagged in real time. None of it has eliminated the temptation entirely — corruption cases still surface in domestic leagues and franchise tournaments around the world — but the culture of silence that let Cronje operate for years no longer holds. Cricket lost its innocence in 2000. What it gained, slowly, was a system built to make sure it wouldn't happen quietly again.
